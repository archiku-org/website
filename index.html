Recognize your software?

Eight situations we hear about every week. If one of them is yours, the question underneath it is the same: do you really know what your software does?

01

The system nobody dares to touch

Somewhere in your organisation runs software that everything depends on and nobody understands anymore. Its makers are gone, its documentation is dead, and every change risks the unknown. What your team needs is an independent map of what the system really does: the system's functional essence, traceable to the exact line of code, delivered in days instead of months of code reading. Too critical to touch becomes understood, and ready to decide about.

02

The delivery you need to verify

A third party built or rebuilt software for you. The contract ended, the warranty is running out, or the acquisition is approaching. Did you get what you paid for? What you need is an independent as-built analysis: what was actually built, how it works, where it deviates from the plan. No stake in the answer, every finding traceable to the code. And afterwards, someone who keeps watching what changes in the repo and reports it to you, not to the builder.

03

The requirements nobody checked the code against

You have a functional analysis. You have requirements. But the code has been changing since the day they were written, and nobody can say they still agree. What you need is an independent comparison of the system as it is actually built against your functional analysis and requirements, every deviation reported and traceable to the code. And then every future change checked against the same bar. Conformance stops being an assumption.

04

The codebase your team ships every week

Development is alive. Releases go out every week. But nobody outside the team, and honestly not even inside it, can say what the system has become. What you need is a baseline of what the system is today, and an independent watcher that analyzes every push against that baseline, so drift is captured while it happens. Progress, quality and conformance, reported to you, not just to the team.

05

The estate you just inherited

You have just taken over the IT estate, as CIO, CTO or new owner. Nobody can hand you the overview: what systems exist, what they actually do, which ones carry the risk. What you need is that overview, built system by system and kept in one place, up to date: a living picture of what your organisation really runs. Your first hundred days, grounded in facts instead of war stories.

06

The team that starts from nothing

A new team is taking over. Insourcing after the vendor left, key people gone, or an acquisition folded in. They inherit the code, the deadlines and zero context. What they need is a validated baseline analysis to start from, instead of months of code reading, and a place where they can ask their questions from day one. Your team is productive in weeks, not quarters.

07

The decision you cannot make yet

Modernize, migrate, wrap or leave it. Every option paper assumes somebody knows what the system does. What you need is the insight the decision needs, in days at a fixed fee, before you commit a budget. Decide on facts, not on folklore.

08

The regulator who asks what your systems do

Under DORA, financial entities must assess their legacy ICT systems every year. Under NIS2, management is personally liable for security and can only evidence what is documented. An undocumented estate is a governance liability. What you need is the documented, traceable baseline these obligations ask for, and for it to stay current, so next year's assessment starts from knowledge, not from panic.

Meet the team

Archiku is not a toolkit. It is a team: four agents and one vault, each with its own craft, all in service of one thing. The truth about your software. Carter reads the code, Harper reads the people, Argus watches it all, Oracle speaks from the Vault. And the Vault remembers.

Carter

Carter is a true excavator. He digs through your code and any existing documentation, layer by layer, oldest evidence first, until the original intent surfaces again.

Hand Carter a system, a delivery or an entire estate and the result is the same: a map of what it really does, traceable to the exact line of code. Methodical, thorough and never in a hurry to judge.

Harper

Harper loves to listen to people. Code does not remember everything, people do. Harper sits down with your people, in their language, and gathers the testimony the code cannot give: decisions taken, reasons forgotten, knowledge that only lives in heads.

What Harper hears is checked against what Carter finds, and the gaps in the analysis close. The listener of the team.

Argus

Argus is our watchman. A hundred eyes, never all closed at once. He watches the codebase as it lives: every push, every change, every drift away from the baseline.

What Argus sees is reported in language a board can act on. And it is Argus who keeps the Vault's knowledge up to date, so the issues it surfaces reach you before they become surprises.

Oracle

Oracle is your trustworthy counsel. An analysis you cannot question is just a document. Oracle answers, straight from the Vault, in the language of the asker: technical for developers, business language for the board, regulatory language for compliance.

Every answer carries the same traceability as the analysis it comes from. Consult the Oracle whenever reading code is not the job at hand.

The Vault

The Vault is a true memory. It is the steady state at the centre of the team. It holds the universal truth of what your software really is: every finding, every citation, every validated insight, in one place. It does not dig and it does not watch. It holds, it remembers, and because Argus never stops watching, it never goes stale. Code enters the perimeter, knowledge is all that comes out, and what the Vault holds outlives every engagement, every vendor and every reorganisation.
