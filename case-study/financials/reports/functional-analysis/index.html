    Functional Analysis

    1. Executive summary

    One household's cashflow books, built so every recorded fact can be proven rather than trusted — an evidence-first journal where changes are appended and balances are recomputation, never edited states.

    The books prove themselves: every correction and removal is its own appended record carrying what changed, each row carries a hashed self-check, and re-landing the same movement stores it once (A correction is recorded, not typed) — judge any change near the journal against this property first.

    Rule-occurrence confirmation can double-book: a group confirm skips an occurrence a real booking already covers, while the same occurrence confirmed by its own address commits again (A covered rule occurrence double-books when confirmed directly by its own address) — aligning that check across both confirm doors is the highest-value fix.

    The removal ritual is not uniform: the expect remove door takes an id and deletes without showing the row or asking for a yes (The expect remove door drops promises without printing or confirming), one door away from the identical guarded operation.

    Which side wins when a retraction record no longer matches the live books is decided nowhere (Refused retraction on journal-versus-books mismatch — which side should win?) — an owner ruling is pending, and until then the keeper facing the refusal has no recovery door.

    Next: read the SWOT analysis for the evidence behind each point and the product summary for the capability map. Verification: six objections were raised during the analysis and all six carry resolved markers at their target versions — no fact stands contested; the vault holds no separate coverage or run record.

    2. Archetype mapping

    The product is a personal bookkeeping journal — one keeper's recurring answers to "did the money move, and what is left?" — built so every recorded answer can be proven rather than trusted. The founding distinction is actual versus expected (Actual versus expected): a movement that happened enters the journaled books, carries and shifts real balances, and is guarded against tampering; a movement promised for a future day weighs nothing, lives in its own register, and can still fail to happen. Nearly every rule in the territory follows from asking that one question.

    The distinction organizes the component map. The journal engine (Ledger engine) folds each accepted change into dated transaction rows whose running balances chain from the row before, so the books are always a recomputation of recorded changes, never a state edited in place; the command pipeline (Command mutations) is the narrow write step every change passes. Intake (Entry intake) gates statements before anything lands, and the future-day cut (Future-day admission) sends tomorrow's money to the promise register (Expected register) instead of the books. The confirmation desk (Open-entry confirmation desk) is where a promise becomes a fact — the confirmation backlog (The confirmation backlog) is the gap between the two calendars, computed fresh at every view and never stored.

    The repair side honours the same proven-over-trusted stance: the retraction desk (Entry retraction) takes a wrong entry out as its own written record that carries what was removed, and the editor (Entry editor) resolves one address across all four stores — books, promises, groups, rule occurrences — walking the keeper field by field before deciding where the corrected content belongs.

    Anticipation is a second promise machinery. The recurrence planner (Recurrence planner) turns repeating history into standing schedules, but every detection result stays a proposal — information on screen, never commitment (Proposal) — and an occurrence carries no stored address at all: the same rule on the same date always derives the same address (The derivation of a projected occurrence's address), which is why a suppressed occurrence can still answer when named. A rule enters the books two ways — written by hand the moment the keeper recognizes an obligation, or adopted from history the books themselves reveal (Rule provenance — written by hand or adopted from history) — and both doors end at the same owner-checked form, so no way in skips the keeper's hands.

    3. Product summary

    The product keeps one household's cashflow books for a single keeper: it turns stated money movements into recorded facts the books stand behind, and every later question — what is left, what was wrong, what is still owed — reads from those facts rather than from an edited state. Recording a movement is one appended change; the books recompute a balance for any day from the change chain, each recorded row carries a self-check over its own content, and a hand-altered book fails verification instead of agreeing quietly (Every entry lands with balances it stands behind). Stating the same movement twice stores it once (Recording twice changes nothing the second time), and a transfer between the household's two accounts shifts both running balances through one entry (A transfer moves both accounts).

    Movements are stated in three precisions — every detail flagged, a Dutch quick shorthand in one breath, or field by field — and all three pass the same admission gate: a real calendar day, words that say what happened, a category the keeper approved, an amount that is not zero, no date beyond today (One admission gate for every stated movement). A rehearsal shows the would-be balances and stores nothing (A rehearsal stores nothing); backing out at any prompt leaves the books untouched (Backing out leaves the books untouched); and a shorthand token that looks like an amount must parse as one or the whole statement is refused (The quick statement must mean what it says).

    Money promised for a future day never enters the balance chain (Future promises never enter the balance chain): it parks in a separate register of open promises until its day arrives. Confirming then turns the promise into a booked fact exactly as it was promised, with no further questions (A landed open item confirms as it was promised); related promises confirm as one group, oldest first, each member re-pointed as it becomes a fact (Confirming a group commits its landed members and keeps the group), and a promise without an amount is refused everywhere (An open item without an amount is never confirmed).

    When something is wrong, three repair desks take over. The correction desk records the fix rather than retyping it: one appended written record, nothing overwritten (A correction is recorded, not typed); an entry restated for a future day leaves the books and re-enters the promise register (An edit that lands tomorrow leaves the books). The retraction desk prints the victim row first and writes only on a typed affirmative answer (A retraction only writes after a stated yes), as one record carrying the removed content (A retraction is one record carrying the entry as it stood). No undo or restore comes after a retraction (No undo or restoration door after a retraction) — the shown-then-confirmed ritual is the safety, by design.

    For payments that repeat, the product reads real payment history and proposes standing schedules; detection alone never saves a rule (Detection never saves a rule): every adopted value passes the keeper's hands in a form walk (Every adopted value passes the owner's hands), and only complete, admissible rules are saved (Only complete admissible rules are saved). A standing schedule never books anything by itself — it only makes the forecast anticipate (Hand-writing a rule never books anything); a real payment recorded in its window takes the scheduled entry's place, so a day never carries two truths.

    6. Concepts

    Actual versus expected

    The founding distinction: a movement that happened (journaled, balance-carrying) versus a movement promised for a future day (register-held, balance-free).

    Future-day admission

    Tomorrow's money is a promise kept apart from today's money: the two sides of today each have their own door, their own idiom and their own checks.

    The confirmation backlog

    The domain term for what is "geland maar niet bevestigd" — landed in real life but not yet accepted into the books; the projected open list is its only ledger.

    Proposal

    A recurring pattern the books found in real history — offered for review, carrying values the owner may keep or replace, but never itself a rule.

    The derivation of a projected occurrence's address

    A standing rule's occurrence carries no stored address: its identifier is always recomputed from the rule's values and the date, which makes it both permanent and schedule-bound.

    Rule provenance — written by hand or adopted from history

    A standing schedule can enter the books two ways — the owner writes it the moment they recognize an obligation, or history reveals the pattern and the owner adopts a proposal — and each provenance carries its own trust story.

    7. Business Rules

    One admission gate for every stated movement

    A money movement enters the books only when its date reads as a real calendar day, its description is not empty, its category is on the approved list, its amount is not zero, and its date is not beyond today — the same checks on every path a movement can be stated.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/entry.py/_validate

    Future promises never enter the balance chain

    A movement dated after today is not recorded as a realized transaction; it is redirected to the expected register until the day it becomes real.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/entry.py/add_transaction#215-254, file/src/financials/commands.py/cmd_add

    A rehearsal stores nothing

    A rehearsal of a movement shows exactly what would be recorded, including the projected balances, and stores nothing.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/entry.py/add_transaction#261-267

    Backing out leaves the books untouched

    An owner who abandons a statement partway — a quit answer at any prompt, a rejection of the balance agreement, or an interrupt — records nothing.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/entry.py/_prompt#154-177

    Every entry lands with balances it stands behind

    A stored movement names the checking and savings balances that result after it, chained from the nearest entry on or before its date, and shifts every later entry by the same amount.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/journal.py/Ledger.insert, file/src/financials/journal.py/Ledger.propagate

    Recording twice changes nothing the second time

    Stating the same movement twice stores it once: a repeat of an already-recorded movement returns the existing entry instead of a second copy.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/journal.py/AddMutation.apply_on#251-254

    A transfer moves both accounts

    When the stated category names a movement between the owner's own accounts, the amount leaves the checking account and arrives in savings mirrored, so both running balances shift by the same entry.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/commands.py/postings_for#88-95

    The quick statement must mean what it says

    In a quick shorthand statement, a token written like an amount is read as an amount or the whole statement is refused — it never degrades into description text.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/shorthand.py/parse_shorthand#114-127

    Only complete future-dated statements enter the expected register

    A movement is parked in the expected register only when its date reads as a real calendar day, its description is not empty, its category is on the approved list, its amount is present, and its date lies after today.

    implemented in: file/src/financials/expected.py/add_expected#65-84

    A zero-amount promise is admitted, an amountless one refused

    An expected statement with no amount at all is refused, but an expected statement whose amount is exactly zero is admitted as a promise.

    8. Process Flows

    Record a money movement in the journaled ledger

    Trigger: A payment or income became known to the owner and must enter the books — surface: the add command (flagged, shorthand or bare interactive)

    The owner notices money has moved — a bill paid, income arrived — and states it in whatever precision the moment gives: fully flagged, a quick shorthand breath like "gisteren -25 Kafe", or field by field when asked. However the statement arrives, one admission gate stands before anything is stored — a real calendar day, words saying what happened, a category on the approved list, a non-zero amount, and no date after today — One admission gate for every stated movement — and a refusal is spoken plainly, the books left exactly as they were. The quick statement is held to mean what it says: a token written like an amount either reads as one or the whole line is turned down, never quietly absorbed into the description — The quick statement must mean what it says. A statement dated ahead of today is never recorded as fact; it redirects to the expected register as a promise — Future promises never enter the balance chain — and Registering a future payment picks that thread up from there. When fields stay open, the intake shows the checking balance the movement would produce and asks for agreement; three disagreements abandon the whole movement, and a quit, an abort or an interrupt records nothing — Backing out leaves the books untouched. A rehearsal behaves the same way in miniature, showing the entry and its projected balances while storing nothing — A rehearsal stores nothing. An accepted movement lands as an entry the books stand behind: the row slots into date order, takes over the balances of the nearest prior day, and shifts every later day by the same amount, so any balance read from the books is the arithmetic of the chain up to that day — Every entry lands with balances it stands behind. Stating the same movement twice within one creation moment stores it once; the second telling returns the first entry — Recording twice changes nothing the second time. Money moved between the owner's own accounts rides a single entry that touches both accounts, so the household total stands still while the split follows the real move — A transfer moves both accounts. The owner hears back the stored identity and the running balances — or the plain reason nothing changed.

    Register a future payment in the expected register

    Trigger: An upcoming payment or income became known and lies ahead of today — surface: the expect command, or the add command when the entered date is in the future

    The rent lands next Tuesday, the salary is on its way, and the owner wants both visible without touching the real balance. Stating a future movement — amount, day, and what it is for — parks a promise in the expected register: every view shows it as an open item, actual money stays untouched, and the promise waits for its day. Two doors lead there. The expect command takes a fully stated movement on purpose, and the ordinary add command funnels into the same register whenever the stated day lies ahead of today — the books will not record a future movement as if it had already happened. Only a complete statement is parked. The register checks that the day reads as a real calendar day, the description says what is coming, the category is on the approved list, and an amount was stated at all; any shortfall earns a plainly worded refusal with nothing stored — Only complete future-dated statements enter the expected register — while a promised zero passes exactly where the actual ledger would refuse one — A zero-amount promise is admitted, an amountless one refused. The today-boundary runs double-stranded too: the register wants a promise ahead of today and the ledger enforces the same divide from its side, so money that has already moved can never end up posing as a promise. Nothing is written blind — a rehearsal shows precisely what would be stored and stores nothing. Once parked the promise carries its own identity and awaits its day; confirmation, told in a story of its own, turns it into a real ledger entry and the cycle closes there.

    Confirm a landed open entry into the books

    Trigger: An expected or projected movement has landed in the account and the owner accepts it into the books — surface: the confirm command (bare, e-number, rule instance or group id)

    Money has moved, and the books must now say so. The owner accepts a landed entry — named by its address, or picked from the desk's open list when nothing was named — and it commits without a single prompt, because every detail was settled when the promise was made (a landed open item confirms as promised). The posting enters as a real journal entry with running balances on both accounts, and the promise retires behind it, its old address kept on the booking's own record.

    Some doors stay shut. Money that has not moved yet is refused and left open — a promise cannot become history ahead of its day (confirmation never lands ahead of the real day), and a movement with no stated amount cannot be booked at all (no amount, no confirmation). An address the books already hold confirms as a quiet no-op, safe to re-run with no double entry (already committed confirms as nothing to do), and a transfer confirmation warns that both accounts moved together.

    Whole groups clear in one pass. Landed members commit in day order, each retired member's address re-pointing to its committed one, while still-future members are skipped with a note and the group itself stands (a group commits its landed members and keeps the group). When the landed item traces to a standing rule, a booking that drifted from the rule's mark — a different amount, or a day outside the window — is recorded as an exception for the owner to judge; an on-mark booking supersedes the occurrence (off-mark bookings count as exceptions). The owner hears the outcome with the new account state.

    Correct a posted entry through the journal

    Trigger: A posted or expected entry shows a mistake and the owner revises it — surface: the edit command (id, or pick from the last ten rows)

    A posted entry shows a mistake — an amount typo, the wrong category, a day from another month — and the owner picks the entry by address, or from the last ten rows when no address springs to mind. The editor reads that address first: it may name a committed book row, a promise for the future, one occurrence of a standing rule, or a whole group. Groups and rule occurrences are corrected in another editor entirely, so this walk serves only the books and the promises register; an address matching nothing ends in an unknown-address refusal, and nothing changed. On a committed row the fields come one at a time — day, description, category, amount — each pre-filled with the value standing now. Enter keeps a value; three failed answers on a field abandon the whole correction, again with nothing changed. Correcting a row to a day after today moves it out of the books: the row is backed out and its corrected content is reserved as a promise for the new day, and the owner is told plainly when that reservation is refused — the tomorrow rule. A promise whose day has already arrived commits into the books in the same act and retires from the register — the landed-promise rule. Every committed repair keeps history intact: the fix lands as a new appended record behind the old content — the record-not-typed rule — and the balance chain re-applies past it, so every later row reflects the fix while the books still show what the entry said before. The owner leaves with the new checking balance; a transfer touched along the way warns that the savings side moved with it.

    Commit a recurring rule instance into the ledger

    Trigger: A recurring bill's occurrence lands in the account and the owner books it — surface: the confirm command on a rule instance id, edit on the instance, or delete as suppression

    A standing rule promises the same bill every month; this is where one month's promise becomes money. The owner brings the projected occurrence to a door, and the plain case is the confirmation desk: hand it an occurrence whose day has landed and it posts the movement exactly as the rule promised, no questions. A day still ahead can be walked through first, every value offered prefilled, the day shifted to when the money actually landed. Two doors reshape rather than book. An occurrence that will differ becomes a one-off promise of its own while the rule skips just that month — but the reshaping door refuses a promise dated today, the one date the doors disagree on, so an owner moving the day to today loses the typing. For a month that should never book at all, a waived rent or a dead subscription, one typed yes is enough and the forecast goes quiet. The desk then scores the posting against the rule's mark — the right category, essentially the promised amount, near the promised day — and either the month counts as handled or the drift is recorded as the rule's exception and stops nagging. Suppression itself leaves no trail in the books, only silence; and a suppressed occurrence still answers to its own address, so the same month can walk back through the booking door.

    Author a standing recurrence rule by hand

    Trigger: The owner recognizes a repeating obligation and formalizes it as a standing rule — surface: the recurrence add, pause, resume and remove commands

    The owner sometimes knows an obligation before the books ever see it: a fresh subscription, a rent change, a yearly insurance premium. Authoring a rule turns that knowledge into a standing schedule directly — one complete statement of description, category, amount and rhythm, or a walk whose questions ask only for what is missing, where pressing Enter keeps the offered answer.

    Two quiet mechanics shape what gets stored. A weekly or biweekly rhythm named by weekday moves the start forward to the first matching day, and every occurrence then lands on that weekday for the schedule's life. No bar compares the start with today: a blank start becomes the authoring day, and a readable past start passes just as freely as a future one — the owner may backdate a rule that was truly running before it was written down.

    Before anything stores, the rule meets the same admission bars as an adopted schedule: a non-empty description, a category the owner approved, a non-zero amount, one of four rhythms, sane day and month ranges, readable dates, and an end never before the start. A refusal names its bar, and the store stays untouched. The saved schedule then confirms itself with the cadence in plain words and the next three dates the forecast will anticipate — yet books nothing. The ledger carries no entry for a rule until a real payment or an explicit confirmation touches it.

    Later life runs through the same store's doors, with the same care. Pause silences a schedule while every field and its history stay stored, and resume brings it back as it stood (paused rules keep their place); removal ends the rule permanently, and only after the owner sees the exact rule and types yes (removal waits for a stated yes). One habit to watch: pressing Enter on the anchor question keeps a silent default, pinning a weekly schedule to whatever weekday the authoring day fell on — the printed cadence line is the only place that shows it.

    Adopt detected recurring rules from ledger history

    Trigger: Repeating payments have accumulated in the ledger history and the owner acts on them — surface: the recurrence detect and recurrence take commands

    The ledger already holds the habit; this flow makes the books say so. Only settled, fully described payments feed the grouping — Only settled history feeds detection — so every proposal names money that actually moved. Payments the owner has already formalized stay silent (A schedule with an existing equal is never re-proposed), which keeps the list a to-do that shrinks as habits are adopted. And because detection is read-only (Detection never saves a rule), the owner can ask for the list as often as they like without ever changing what the books anticipate.

    Two tiers of answer come back. A steady payment — the rent, the subscription whose figure barely moves — proposes its median outright (A steady-amount payment proposes its median). A budget-style payment that keeps its appointment for changing figures proposes the same middle value but carries its observed low and high openly (A timely varying-amount payment proposes its median with its spread), so the owner sees the spread at a glance. A payment that swings between income and expense is refused as one habit (A payment habit mixing income and expense is never one pattern).

    Nothing is created by looking. Naming a proposal opens a form pre-filled with the detected values; the owner accepts each field or retypes it, and quitting at any point stores nothing. Only a completed walk that passes the admission checks saves the rule (Only complete admissible rules are saved) — refused rules come back with their reason and the books stay untouched. A saved rule immediately shows its next three anticipated dates, and from then on the forecast generates its instances without further typing.

    Retract a wrong entry from the books with confirmation

    Trigger: An owner judges an entry or projected instance wrong and retracts it — surface: the delete command (id, or pick from the last ten rows)

    An owner catches an entry that should never have hit the books — a duplicate, or something that simply is not real. The delete command takes the id by name, or offers the last ten posted rows to pick from. The chosen row prints first, and nothing moves until the owner types a clear yes; a bare Enter or any other word leaves the books untouched.

    The kind of wrongness sets the shape. A committed entry never vanishes in place: its removal lands in the journal as one record carrying the entry's full prior state, so the books can be built up again and prove exactly what left — and every later balance re-reads as if the row had never posted. A promised future payment is dropped from its register, since it never weighed on the books. A deleted group takes only the grouping with it, the member entries staying put. A projected occurrence goes quiet for one date while its rule keeps running. An address the books cannot resolve ends the attempt with nothing written at all.

    When the removed entry moved money between two accounts, the retraction warns the owner to re-check both sides. One neighbouring door stays unguarded: the promises register's standalone remove action drops a promise without printing it or asking.

    12. Non-Goals

    No undo or restoration door after a retraction

    Statement: The system deliberately provides no restore or undo for a removed entry — recovery means re-registering, and only the journal's own records preserve what the retraction carried.

    Rationale: Once an entry is retracted, nothing offers to put it back. There is no restore command, no trash, no second-chance door. The deliberate boundary shows in the design everywhere else: retraction demands a printed row and a typed yes precisely because there is no take-back afterwards; the retraction record itself carries the removed content so the journal COULD reconstruct what was removed, but the product exposes no command that does so for the owner.

    The owner's recovery path is the general one: re-register what was wrongly removed (through the intake or the promises register, whichever fits), accepting that a fresh registration is a new record, not the old one restored. A projected occurrence excluded by mistake can be brought back by editing the rule's exceptions — again through the rules machinery, not a restore door.

    The boundary holds because the confirmation ritual makes the no-undo stance liveable: the owner was shown and asked, twice over on the recent-picker, before anything was written.

    Suppression stores no suppression record

    Statement: Deleting a projected occurrence writes nothing that remembers the suppression — no flag, note or row — it only adds the date to the rule's exception list, so the forecast stops expecting money it silently no longer shows. Rationale: When the owner deletes a projected occurrence, the stores change in one way only: the date joins the rule's exempted dates. No suppression flag rides the rule, no note explains the decision, and no entry in any book names the suppressed occurrence. Whoever later asks "why is there no rent in March?" — the owner months on, or anyone reading the books — finds nothing: the rule simply projects everything except the dates it was told to skip.

    The boundary is deliberate within this door: suppression is forecast-silencing, not bookkeeping. A decision that must be explainable later has to take a different door (booking the real movement, or reshaping the occurrence into a promise with its own record).

    Hand-writing a rule never books anything

    Statement: The system deliberately does not record a ledger entry, change any balance or store an instance when a standing schedule is written — the schedule only ever shows up as anticipation the forecast presents. Rationale: The moment a standing rule is saved, the household's real balances are untouched. The rule exists so the forecast can anticipate the payment — and only so the forecast can. The module that stores rules states this boundary explicitly: rules are never materialized into the transaction store; each repeats only as a virtual expected entry during a viewing window, and a real recorded payment in that window takes its place so a day never carries two truths. Whatever the owner later wants booked must still travel the normal recording doors; a rule is a promise, not a payment.

    13. Quirks

    The dry run projects only the checking account (parity)

    Description: A rehearsal of a transfer of, say, €250 shows the checking side (−250) and its projected result, but not the mirrored +250 on savings, although the real landing would move both. The dry-run line the owner sees is built from the checking posting and the projected checking balance alone. The rehearsal still stores nothing, so nothing is wrong in the books — but the owner weighing the rehearsal against reality sees half of the movement.

    Parity Relevance: A faithful reimplementation should either mirror this preview asymmetry exactly or knowingly fix it; a dry-run parity test with a transfer category would expose the difference.

    Impact: file/src/financials/entry.py/add_transaction#261-267

    An identical restatement depends on when it was entered (integrity)

    Description: The deduplication the owner might assume from 'recording twice changes nothing' only holds within one timestamp. The mutation's identity mixes content with its creation moment, deliberately — the comment on the field says as much: uniqueness is a human discipline, the timestamp exists only to separate identical content. So re-adding the same café visit an hour later is honest from the tool's reading — identical content, different creation moment, therefore a different movement. The owner who meant to correct and accidentally re-added now has the movement twice, and only the later delete/edit flow repairs it.

    Parity Relevance: Idempotency tests must control timestamps: the same payload with the same ts folds, with a different ts duplicates.

    Impact: file/src/financials/journal.py/Mutation#62-65, file/src/financials/journal.py/AddMutation.apply_on#251-254

    Backdated entries are inserted, later ones rewritten (lineage)

    Description: Add 'yesterday's café' a week after the fact and the row slots between its neighbors, shifting each later day's balances by the posting — the chain never shows the recording moment on the row itself. The creation timestamp of the mutation preserves the stating order in the journal, but the ledger rows read as if the movement had always been in date order. That is the intended bookkeeping behavior, but a reader comparing two books rebuilt at different times will find balances that match per-row yet disagree if the earlier rebuild predates a backdated insert: the checkpoint carries the rewrite, and the journal carries the true order of statements.

    Parity Relevance: A reimplementation must reproduce insert-position semantics and balance rewriting, not append-order — tests that only append will pass both designs and hide the difference.

    Impact: file/src/financials/journal.py/Ledger.insert#206-218, file/src/financials/journal.py/Ledger.propagate

    An incomplete add statement keeps its future dispatch hidden until prompted complete (ux)

    Description: A future-dated movement stated in one breath — every field given — is redirected to the expectation register immediately, before anything else happens. But when the owner states the movement only in pieces, the redirect waits hidden behind the interactive questions: the owner first answers every field, and only the completed statement decides which door takes it. During those questions a future date handed to the checking balance position is treated as a balance number, not as a date — nothing about the statement hints that the register is where it will end up. The owner answers the usual prompts and the entry parks as a promise only after the last answer.

    Parity Relevance: Impact: file/src/financials/entry.py/add_transaction#215-253

    The dry-run confirmation names an id the entry never keeps (parity)

    Description: Rehearsing an expectation shows the owner exactly what would be parked, down to an identifier the entry would carry. Nothing is stored, yet the message reads like a completed booking: the owner can walk away believing the promise is registered. When the statement is actually parked afterwards — often days later — the register hands out identifiers by current order, so another promise entered in between may take the very id the rehearsal displayed. The number in the rehearsal message is a preview of a moving target, not a binding commitment.

    Parity Relevance: Impact: file/src/financials/expected.py/add_expected#105-110

    An unknown rule-instance address fails the confirm in silence (ux)

    Description: Naming the confirmation desk with an address from the rule-instance family that matches no standing rule produces no visible answer at all — no explanation, no refusal note, only a failure exit. The code's own remark says the unknown id is the caller's business to report, yet the address reaches the desk through exactly that caller, which delegates and returns without a word. Every other unknown address greets the owner with a clear refusal. The gap is invisible in the direct case and untested; a typo in a remembered instance address simply looks like the confirm did nothing.

    Parity Relevance: A parity re-implementation that reports unknown r: ids like every other unknown id would differ observably from this desk (exit code matches, user-visible output does not).

    Impact: file/src/financials/confirm.py/_confirm_instance#108-109, file/src/financials/confirm.py/confirm_transaction#250-251

    All-Enter keeps a posted row but confirms a landed promise (ux)

    Description: The editor's fields all keep their current value when confirmed unchanged, which reads as a pure review gesture: look, leave everything as it stands, move on. On a posted row that reading is right — confirming everything answers "nothing changed" and no record is written. On a promise whose day has already arrived the same gesture IS a confirmation: the books ask no further question and the row is committed as a landed fact as the owner steps out of the walk. The identical action thus means "I changed nothing" on one desk and "this happened as promised" on the other, with no signal at the moment of acting that the second case is a commit at all.

    Parity Relevance: A parity port must reproduce the dual meaning exactly and preserve the no-second-question character of the landed-promise path; making the confirm explicit would change an owner-visible flow. Impact: file/src/financials/edit.py/_edit_expected#147-152, file/src/financials/edit.py/_edit_actual#245-247

    The expect remove door drops promises without printing or confirming (ux)

    Description: The observable surprise: an owner who has learned that every removal in this product first shows the entry and demands a typed yes meets one door where none of that happens. Removing a future-dated promise through the expect command's remove action takes the id and deletes — no row printed, no confirmation asked — announcing only the success after the fact; even a wrong id gets only the same bare unknown-id error the guarded doors print.

    The same promise removed through the retract command instead goes through the full ritual: printed row, explicit 'ja' or cancel. Two doors, one operation, two different guarantees. The register's own add side refuses incompleteness and the past loudly, so the asymmetry is not a general laxness of the register but a one-door-only gap.

    Parity Relevance: Impact: file/src/financials/expected.py/remove_expected, file/src/financials/delete.py/_delete_expected

    Weekly proposals anchor on the first occurrence's weekday (format)

    Description: The owner sees weekly proposals shown with a weekday in brackets — that weekday is not asked, not chosen, and not stored per instance: it is inherited from the very first date the detection trusted. Adoption seeds the same date as the start, so accepting everything keeps that weekday forever, and the instance calendar repeats it from then on.

    The surprise cuts in when history was irregular: the earliest recorded occurrence may sit on a different weekday than the payment's current habit (a payment that fell on Friday the first time and Tuesday since). The proposal carries the Friday, and nothing in the review or adoption names the difference as a warning. Only typing a bare weekday name in the form's anchor question moves the schedule off that inherited day.

    This is why the form asks a weekday at all for weekly rhythms — the anchor question exists to let the owner override exactly this inherited value, and Enter silently ratifies whatever the first occurrence decided.

    Parity Relevance: Impact: file/src/financials/recurrences.py/_proposal#288-303, file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/detect_recurrences#886-890

    The projection hides a suppressed occurrence but its address stays bookable (ux)

    Description: The owner deletes an occurrence and sees it vanish — the forecast keeps its word. Nothing, though, was recorded about the address: it derives from the rule's schedule only, and no suppression flag exists to find. So the same address, typed later at the confirm desk or the edit desk, still resolves to rule and occurrence and offers the same booking or reshaping walk as before. A suppressed occurrence can therefore be confirmed after all, entering the books on a date the projection had already given up.

    The code documents this deliberately: the resolver's own note says suppressed instances stay resolvable and the delete door never warns that the disappearance is forecast-only. For the owner the oddity is exactly the double-booking shape of the covered-occurrence sensitivity, reachable after a deliberate delete.

    Parity Relevance: Impact: file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/_find_instance#575-586, file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/_find_instance#587-593

    15. Sensitivity

    The projection preview and the real write share one predecessor

    The checking balance the owner is asked to agree with, and the balance the stored entry will actually carry, both come from the same date-predecessor computation — if that ever diverges, the owner signs off on a balance the books will not hold.

    Found in: file/src/financials/commands.py/projected_checking#179-192, file/src/financials/journal.py/Ledger.find_parent#187-194

    A promised expense mistyped as zero keeps every forecast quiet

    A future-dated expense stated as zero is admitted by the expectation door and weighs nothing in every forecast, while the same zero statement would have been refused with a plain message at the actual ledger door.

    Found in: file/src/financials/expected.py/add_expected#76-78, file/src/financials/entry.py/_validate#80-81

    The fixes repair door writes promises without the intake checks

    An expected entry created through a cleanup-fixes repair enters the register with no future-date, approved-category, or amount check, so a mis-stated repair lands where the commands would have refused it.

    Found in: file/src/financials/expected.py/add_expected_row_from_fix#33-53

    An amount-less promise can reach the open picker and break the offer to confirm

    The open list offers every landed promise for confirmation regardless of whether its amount was ever recorded, so one amount-less promise silently poisons the very screen the owner uses to confirm everything else. Found in: file/src/financials/ledger_view.py/_compose_projection#123, file/src/financials/confirm.py/_pick_open#226-226

    A covered rule occurrence double-books when confirmed directly by its own address

    Confirming a standing-rule occurrence by its own address commits unconditionally, while the same occurrence inside a group is first checked against bookings that already cover it — the double-booking guard exists on one door only. Found in: file/src/financials/confirm.py/_confirm_instance#114-121, file/src/financials/confirm.py/_confirm_group#183-192, file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/commit_instance#616-622

    The unguarded remove door drops the wrong promise silently

    Anyone removing a promised entry through the unguarded remove door can delete the wrong id with no preview and no confirmation, and hear only a success or a bare unknown-id error — the mistake is silent on both sides.

    Found in: file/src/financials/expected.py/remove_expected

    The adoption re-derives the list the owner reviewed

    The proposal list the owner reviews and the list the adoption consults are two separate detections — a value change in the ledger between review and adoption changes what the adoption offers without ever saying so.

    Found in: file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/detect_recurrences#860-862, file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/take_proposal#921-927

    The detector and the view must agree on what a repeating payment is

    Proposals are computed over the ledger view's rows, so the detector's notion of a settled payment inherits every filtering and naming rule the view layer applies silently.

    Found in: file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/detect_recurrences#858-862

    Deleting an occurrence reads as a booking decision but books nothing

    The owner can trust the forecast's disappearance as proof an occurrence is handled, while the deleted instance remains bookable through its own address — the money can enter the books twice from the owner's point of view. Found in: file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/suppress_occurrence#816-848, file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/commit_instance#616-622

    The same add walk on different days stores different weekly schedules

    An owner who completes the weekday question with Enter gets a schedule anchored to whatever weekday the authoring day happened to be, so re-typing a lost rule on another day silently produces a different schedule than the original. Found in: file/src/financials/recurrence_cli.py/add_recurrence#400-437
