Functional Analysis

1. Executive summary

A personal double-entry ledger with declared intentions — an audit-first household money book where every recorded change appends to one unbroken history and nothing is ever rewritten in place.

Findings a manager should act on:

  1. The books are tamper-evident by construction: every save, correction and removal is an append-only event identified by its own content (the write core), and a deletion carries the removed row's complete picture, verified against the live row before it applies (the pre-image concept). Trust these books and treat any content-mismatch boot refusal as an incident.

  2. Reviewed correction batches are not atomic: a refused entry stops the run with earlier entries already applied and journaled, and recovery is human (the no-rollback boundary). Worse, only the apply-all pass marks a consumed batch file so it cannot apply twice — a single-file application leaves it pending and re-applies the leading entries on every retry (the marking rule). Decide whether that asymmetry is grace or a defect.

  3. Surfaces speak two languages: batch corrections answer in English while every interactive surface answers in Dutch (the batch process). Small, user-facing, worth a decision.

  4. One boundary question is open for the business: an expectation reserved with a deliberate zero amount can confirm into a zero-amount committed row, because confirm blesses values as-is (the correction process). Whether that is gap or grace is not decodable from the behaviour alone — it needs a business ruling.

Next step: read the product summary for the capability-by-capability walk, then the archetype mapping for the kind of application this is — and bring the four findings above to the owners of the books.

Verification status: the vault's objection census holds zero entries — no objections have been captured against this analysis, so these findings stand unchallenged but also unverified by that signal.

2. Archetype mapping

This is a bookkeeper's ledger brought to one person's household: an audit-first money book in the double-entry tradition, joined with a calendar-driven planner for commitments and card statements. The archetype shows in three disciplines the product never breaks.

The first discipline is the unbroken history. Nothing recorded is ever rewritten: a movement, a correction, a removal — each appends its own event to the running books, each event identified by what it says (the journaled write core), with the saved position and its checkpoint explained in the checkpoint concept. The saldo on any row is a chain(the running balance) — every row continues the row dated before it — which is why a wrong balance is always repaired by correcting an entry, never by touching the balance itself.

The second discipline is the wall between plan and fact. Money dated ahead of today lives in the register of expectations with no influence on balances; when its day comes, the confirm moment blesses it into history exactly as stated — the confirmable open entry in its three shapes. Openstaand is the middle ground that keeps landed-but-unconfirmed movements visible until the owner decides, and the projectie walks only declared plans forward — nothing statistical.

The third discipline is commitments stated once. The recurrence rule never becomes a book row: its occurrences are computed fresh at every viewing (the rule engine), each occurrence carrying a content-derived identity so the owner can name, confirm or suppress one date anywhere it appears. Where a real booking already covers the rule's period, the real row wins.

Around these disciplines the rest of the archetype is naming and grouping. The four-kind id resolver lets every entry — committed row, expectation, group, projected date — be addressed by its id wherever the owner is (the edit resolver, the removal front desk). A group is bookkeeping metadata, not money (the group registry): it exists so a card statement reads as one rollup row whose total lands on the payment day. Even outside corrections, reviewed batch files ride the same audited write step (the fixes engine).

So the kind of application this is: a personal double-entry ledger with declared intentions — an appendix-style command console whose trust rests on auditability, whose convenience rests on every view being derivable anew from one authoritative history.

3. Product summary

This product is a household bookkeeping console the owner drives from a terminal: money movements, standing commitments and card statements become one auditable money picture with a forecast attached.

Recording a movement is a single act — fully typed options, a one-line phrase, or an interactive walk where anything unstated is asked and nothing is assumed (the two command doors, the intake flow). The category is chosen from the approved list, never typed freely (the approved-list picker), the quick-add phrase refuses ambiguous money rather than guessing it (the shorthand parser), and nothing is stored before the entry has passed the household's checks.

Standing commitments — rent, salary, subscriptions — are stated once as a rule and never re-typed: each period their occurrences stand in the open view until the bill really lands (the rule engine, the rule maintenance surface). A rule is defined through the same questions whether typed by hand or adopted from a proposal, and the detector only ever proposes from the books' own history, creating nothing until the owner adopts it field by field (the definition flow, the detection flow).

When forecast money has really landed, one confirmation blesses it into history exactly as stated, zero prompts — a matured expectation retired into its committed row, a rule occurrence committed with the rule's own values, or a whole statement's landed members walked in date order (the confirm flow, the commit core). Confirm adjusts nothing and refuses money dated ahead of today; changing values belongs to the edit flow.

Correction runs through one resolver that reads what a named entry is — committed row, open expectation, statement group, projected occurrence — and opens that kind's editor with prompts pre-filled (the edit resolver). Corrections are recorded as their own events and history is never rewritten; moving an entry into the future takes it out of the ledger into the plans, and an id that turns out to name a group opens the group's own editor instead of declining (the group-edit fallback).

Removal knows four directions, each showing the entry in full first and waiting for a typed yes — pressing Enter alone never removes anything (the removal front desk). A committed row leaves the books through its own recorded event carrying the row's complete former picture, with every later balance re-computed from that point (the delete engine); removing a transfer warns that the paired savings chain moved too. Future plans are withdrawn silently, statement groups dissolve leaving every member untouched, and one ruled date is suppressed while the rule keeps running.

Money dated ahead of today is a plan, kept apart in the register of expectations with no effect on any balance until it is confirmed. The register refuses any date that is not genuinely in the future, and it tolerates a deliberate zero amount as a reservation where committed money refuses zero outright.

A card statement's charges bundle into a named group with its own payment date; members join and leave all-or-nothing, and the group answers to exact id, unique prefix, name or unique name prefix (the group registry, the member resolver). In the view the group collapses into one rollup row whose total lands on the payment day, with member ids carried in its note for auditability (the rollup builder, the view collapse). Deleting a group is an un-group: every member row survives.

Wherever the owner looks, one composed picture serves three windows — the printed list with month-end and year-end balances in its footer, the scrollable interactive view, and the monthly retrospective (the view composition, the interactive ledger, the report). Search terms hide rows for the eye only, never moving balances, and the report measures booked history alone, never counting transfers between the owner's own accounts as income or expense.

Under everything, every recorded change appends to the running event journal with an identity derived from its own content, and the saved position is brought atomically up to date after each save (the write core) — every balance explainable, altered history detectable. Reviewed correction batches from files ride the same audited write step, each entry refusing loudly before its own write when the books cannot safely receive it; a consumed batch file is marked so it cannot apply twice (the fixes engine).

The edges are deliberate: the projection expands only what the owner declared, never statistics (the forecasting boundary); capture looks for no duplicates — an identical re-save is a harmless no-op, a near-duplicate visible to nobody (the capture boundary); removal has no undo beyond the journal itself; and grouping never touches member rows or the money journal. This story rests on the full captured analysis — all 25 components, 16 concepts, 8 non-goals and 8 processes read in whole, with the rule layer as a deterministic sample of 50 of 54 business-rule facts.

4. Components

Category prompt (approved-list picker)

The interactive question that lets the owner choose a category — always from the approved list, never free text — with abort as an explicit outcome.

Command-line entrypoints (financials add and expect)

The two command surfaces of this process — "financials add" for movements at or before today, "financials expect" for future ones — carrying the owner's stated fields into the capture flow.
grounded in: src/financials/cli.py

Commitment maintenance surface — list, pause, resume, remove, edit

The owner's day-to-day control over standing rules — review the rules and their next occurrences, pause or resume them, remove one after a typed confirmation, or edit it through the seeded field loop.

Confirm commit core

The shared write step every confirm path funnels into — one journaled mutation that lands the real movement, stamped and persisted in a single append.
grounded in: src/financials/commands.py

Edit-command fallback into the group editor

When the owner edits something that is neither a committed entry nor an expected row, the edit flow falls back to recognizing a group id and opens the group's interactive editor instead of the transaction editor.
grounded in: src/financials/edit.py

Entry removal resolver and confirmation facade

The front desk of every removal — it figures out which kind of entry is being asked about, shows exactly what is about to disappear, and refuses to act until a typed yes arrives.

grounded in: src/financials/delete.py

Expected register (expected store and rows)

The separate store for money movements dated in the future — validated on save, kept apart from the actual ledger, and carrying no balance influence until confirmed.
grounded in: src/financials/expected.py

Group member resolver across id families

Resolves one member id into the row it names, walking the three id families in order — committed ledger, expected register, and rule instances (including future instances of edited rules) — so a group's membership list can always be re-read into real rows.

Group registry for statement rollups

The register of named statement groups: creating, finding by id or name, membership editing and un-grouping; each group is one line of bookkeeping metadata with its own rollup date and label.
grounded in: src/financials/groups.py

Interactive entry-edit resolver

Resolves an edited entry's kind — committed row, expected row, statement group or projected rule occurrence — and opens the matching interactive prompts.
grounded in: src/financials/edit.py, src/financials/cli.py, src/financials/groups.py

5. Concepts

Checkpoint (the ledger's saved position)

The periodically persisted snapshot of the ledger plus the watermark of how far it reflects the journal — the reason boots are fast and crash catch-up is decidable.

Confirmable open entry

The three shapes an unconfirmed-but-real money movement takes — a registered expectation, a rule occurrence, or a whole statement group — each committed by the same confirm moment.

Dangling member as unresolvable membership id

A member id that no longer resolves to any row — a dropped expected row or an edited rule — which view and confirm flows skip with a note rather than rejecting.

Group as named statement rollup

A group is bookkeeping metadata — not money movement: a named bundling of related rows (id, label, optional rollup date, category, note, member list) that exists only so the view can show one row instead of many.

Group removal as un-grouping

Removing a group statement that only dissolves the bond between member rows — the members themselves stay, which is why the command's promise is "un-group, not delete".

Occurrence suppression (excepting one date of a rule)

The removal variant for projected rule dates: the exact date is added to the rule's exception list so one instance stops being projected while the rule itself keeps running.

Openstaand — landed but not yet confirmed

The middle section of the picture: movements that have arrived but are not yet booked history.

Pre-image (the removed row's full picture)

The complete content of a ledger row as it stood at the moment of removal — recorded inside the removal event so history can both reconstruct and verify the deletion.

Projectie — the forward projection to year end

The money that is expected but has not happened yet: approved rules and one-off expectations walked forward as balances.

Rollup row as the virtual one-row statement

The virtual row standing in for a group's members — folder-prefixed label with member count, the summed amount, the rollup date — shown in place of the individual purchases.

6. Business Rules

A commit that covers the rule's period supersedes the instance

A committed transaction covering the rule's period — same category, amount within tolerance and date within the superseding window — makes the projected instance disappear from the open backlog without any separate action. implemented in: src/financials/recurrences.py:147-182, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:629-639

A committed entry corrected into the future leaves the ledger

When the owner corrects a committed entry's date to a future date, the entry is removed from the ledger and recreated as an expectation at the new date — a committed actual is never stored ahead of today. implemented in: src/financials/edit.py:249-252, src/financials/edit.py:208-236

A confirmed instance date may shift down to today but never ahead

When a rule occurrence is confirmed interactively, the owner may move the confirmation date down — even from a future schedule to today, for a bill that landed early — but a confirmed date in the future is refused, because an actual never lies ahead of today. implemented in: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:649-654, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:669-675

A correction the books cannot safely receive stops the batch before any write

A reviewed correction that names an empty or ambiguous id, an unsupported field or operation, an unknown row, a stale old value, or an unsupported global target aborts with a loud refusal, and no journaled event is written for that entry — so every journal line that exists records a change that actually happened. implemented in: src/financials/fixes.py:127-181, src/financials/fixes.py:184-227, src/financials/fixes.py:61-69

A delete event must match the row it claims to remove

Before a delete event is applied, the row's content recorded in it is checked against the row actually present — a mismatch stops the removal loudly instead of replaying a false history. implemented in: src/financials/journal.py:311-346

A fully committed group collapses into one rollup row

When every member of a group is committed to the ledger, the view shows the group as one total row instead of the individual member rows; a group with any uncommitted member still shows its committed members individually. implemented in: src/financials/ledger_view.py:205-232, src/financials/ledger_view.py:344-367

A future-dated movement goes to the expected register, not the ledger

An entry whose date lies after today is kept in the expected register without balance effect; only today-or-past dates may be stored in the actual ledger. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:215-232, src/financials/entry.py:244-254, src/financials/expected.py:79-84, src/financials/entry.py:82-86

A group confirms only its landed members and survives the confirmation

Confirming a group commits every landed member in date order, skips future, dangling, already-committed and amount-less members with a note, and keeps the group itself alive by re-pointing each member id to its new committed identity. implemented in: src/financials/confirm.py:124-202

A group removal dissolves the bond, never the members

Removing a group statement removes only the grouping itself — every member row survives unchanged in its own right. implemented in: src/financials/groups.py:322-335

A group resolves by exact id, unique id prefix, name or unique name prefix

A group can be addressed by its exact id, by a unique id prefix, by its exact name (case-insensitive) or by a unique name prefix — and an ambiguous prefix matches nothing, keeping ids the canonical address. implemented in: src/financials/groups.py:82-102

7. Process Flows

Apply cleanup fixes

Trigger: Reviewed data corrections are ready as fix files and must reach the ledger. Surface: the financials fix command with a file or the apply-all form.

Confirm landed entry

Trigger: Forecast money movement has landed in reality — a scheduled payment has matured or a statement has arrived, and the owner commits it. Surface: the financials confirm command (by id or picked from the OPEN section).

Correct recorded entry

Trigger: A recorded entry turns out to be wrong — the owner needs to fix history or an open entry. Surface: the financials edit command.

Group statement rows

Trigger: A card statement arrives whose charges need one combined view and rollup. Surface: the financials group command (create, add, remove, edit, show, delete, list).

Manage recurring rules

Trigger: A standing financial commitment needs maintaining, or a recurring bill landed and must be committed. Surface: the financials recurrence command and the rule-instance branches of confirm, edit and delete.

Remove recorded entry

Trigger: An entry must not be in the ledger at all — duplicate capture, test row, or a payment that did not happen. Surface: the financials delete command.

Transaction intake

Trigger: A payment or receipt must be recorded — the owner captures the money movement when it happens. Surface: the financials add command (flags, shorthand, or interactive).

View ledger and forecast

Trigger: The owner wants to see where the money stands — committed history, what is still open, and what the coming period brings. Surface: the financials list command, the financials tui command, and the financials report command.

9. Conventions

Batch-file corrections speak English where the interactive surface speaks Dutch

The fixes engine's user-facing messages — refusals, outcome lines, the pending-scope-empty message — are English, while the interactive capture and correction surfaces print Dutch.
applied in: src/financials/fixes.py:180-237, src/financials/cli.py:264-270

Confirm feedback and refusals speak Dutch

Every user-facing message in the confirm flow (and the CLI broadly) is written in Dutch — success, skip, refusal and prompt alike — while all code identifiers and docstrings are English.

applied in: src/financials/confirm.py:213-228, src/financials/confirm.py:247-258

Dutch user-facing strings with Enter-keeps and cancel grammar

All interactive prompts, confirmations and refusals in the financials program are written in Dutch with a shared grammar — Enter keeps the pre-filled value, 'q' or an empty answer cancels, and refusals start with "Niet opgeslagen" or "Niet".
applied in: src/financials/edit.py:69-102, src/financials/groups.py:343-361

One composition, many renderers

View surfaces compose the ledger picture once into plain data and derive all section breaks from empty-list checks, never from label rows.
applied in: src/financials/ledger_view.py:371-388, src/financials/tui/init.py:323-395

Removal exit codes — 0 acted, 2 refused or unknown

Every removal path returns exit code 0 when it acted (or found its work idempotently done) and 2 when anything blocked action — cancelled consent, unknown id, unresolved group; the CLI raises SystemExit with the code directly.
applied in: src/financials/delete.py:95-143, src/financials/cli.py:310-312

Resolver fallback chains order row families before group ids

The edit and delete flows resolve ids in a fixed order — committed ledger, expected register, group registry, rule instance — with the group registry resolved second-to-last and the last resolver's decline treated as cancel rather than unknown.
applied in: src/financials/delete.py:108-125, src/financials/edit.py:284-301

The registry file is re-read fresh before every group save

Every flow that edits a group loads the registry, mutates its in-memory copy and saves — and the multi-step confirm flow re-reads the registry fresh before each of its saves.
applied in: src/financials/confirm.py:159-170, src/financials/groups.py:57-71

User-facing messages are Dutch

Every owner-facing message (prompts, refusals, warnings, confirmations) across the intake flow is written in Dutch, with a compact refusal shape of "Niet opgeslagen" plus the reason.
applied in: src/financials/entry.py:104-105, src/financials/entry.py:280, src/financials/expected.py:74

11. Non-Goals

A batch never applies all-or-nothing — no atomic rollback of a partial batch

Statement: The fixes engine does not attempt batch atomicity: a refused entry stops the run with earlier entries already applied and journaled, and there is no rollback of whatever landed before the refusal.
Rationale: A batch application is deliberately not a transaction. Each entry passes its guards and lands independently; a refusal wedges the run at that entry with the batch's leading entries already recorded. Nothing in the engine reverts the applied portion — recovery is human: fix the bad entry in the file and re-run, or remove the file. The audit property justifies it: every journal line is a change that really happened, and undoing honestly-written events automatically would hide what occurred. The wedge loop is visible (the file stays pending) rather than silent.

Confirm never adjusts values

Statement: The confirm command deliberately offers no way to change what an entry says — date, description, category and amount are only adjustable through the edit flow, never at confirmation time.

Rationale: The command's help text states the division of labor explicitly: confirm what landed as-is, adjust first with the edit command. The only value confirm ever changes is the date of a future-dated rule instance in the interactive path — shifted down to today, never ahead. Everything else stays exactly as registered or as the rule carries it.

The boundary holds across all three branches and is visible in the refusal messages, which name the edit flow rather than offering a prompt. This is what keeps confirm fast (zero prompts on the landed path) and the books honest (nothing enters history that was not stated first).

Grouping never modifies or deletes member rows

Statement: No group action re-dates, re-categorizes, commits or deletes a member row — membership edits touch only the group's own member list.
Rationale: The boundary is stark on purpose: a member's row always remains the property of its own register (ledger, expected register or rule expansion). Group views collapse and confirm flows walk members, but the only group-side writes are to the membership list. This is the guarantee behind "deleting a group is an un-group" and behind members keeping their ids after confirmation.

Groups are never journaled as money movement

Statement: Grouping does not record money — groups are stored only in their own metadata registry alongside the expected register and recurrence rules, never in the committed-money journal.
Rationale: The money journal answers "what money left or entered, and when" and is append-only for exactly that. A group is not an answer to that question — it is an organizational view over rows that already exist. Storing it outside the journal keeps the bundling editable without touching money history, and keeps the journal purely financial. If a group vanishes or changes, no money record is affected; only the view changes.

No duplicate detection at capture time

Statement: The intake flow does not look for or warn about an entry that looks the same as an existing row; identical content simply re-saves as an idempotent no-op in the engine.
Rationale: Capturing the same receipt twice is the classic double-entry mistake, and the flow does nothing to stop it. Two captures with identical content produce the identical content-derived event id, so the second save applies as a change nothing — the row count does not grow and the balances do not move. A slightly different capture of the same movement (one word different) does produce a second real row, and nothing flags the pair.

This is a deliberate boundary: capture checks only the house rules, not history. The engine's idempotence makes the accidental double-tap invisible; nothing makes the deliberate near-duplicate visible.

No statistical forecasting

Statement: The projection never estimates or extrapolates past spending; it expands only explicitly approved recurring rules and recorded one-off expectations.
Rationale: Recurring entries enter the forecast only through rules the owner approved (or explicitly adopted from detection proposals); one-off future amounts enter only as recorded expectations. Nothing in the view composition invents a row from history's shape — even the recurring-rule detection tool merely proposes, and the owner must adopt a proposal before its future exists. This keeps the forecast auditable: every predicted row traces to a declaration the owner made.

No undo of a removal beyond the journal itself

Statement: The removal process offers no undo command or trash-bin — the only way back is re-entering the row from the history's own record of it, which is a manual re-capture, not a restore.
Rationale: The delete surface knows only four directions, all forwards: delete, withdraw, dissolve, suppress. Nothing re-creates what was removed. The design leans on the audit property instead: because the removal event carries the victim's complete former picture, the information needed to rebuild the row survives — but rebuilding is left to the owner as a fresh capture, with new row identity and fresh balances. This matters for expectations too: an expectation removed by accident is not recoverable by any command; it must be re-created through the planning flow. The absence is deliberate safety architecture, not a gap: an undo feature would need to invert a removal and re-propagate balances, duplicating every risk the pre-image discipline exists to control.

The correction flow never edits account balances

Statement: The edit prompts offer exactly date, description, category and amount — the account balances always recompute from the postings and are never values the owner can set.
Rationale: The owner who sees a saldo they do not recognize cannot reach into it through the correction flow. The prompts carry exactly the four business fields of the entry; the running balances are derived — recomputed from the entries' postings whenever the history is replayed.

The deliberate boundary: a balance that is wrong is wrong because an entry is wrong (or missing), and the repair is correcting that entry — never hand-setting the balance. This keeps the chained saldo internally consistent: if every posting matches its entry, the balances must agree.

12. Appendix: Quirks

A single-file fix leaves the file pending while --all marks it applied (parity)

Description: The apply-once guarantee is asymmetric between the two entry forms. The apply-all pass renames each consumed file with the applied marker, so the same batch cannot reach the books twice through --all. The single-file form applies the named file but leaves its name untouched, so the file stays in the pending scope: a later apply-all run would pick it up and apply the same corrections a second time — where each field correction then behaves idempotently (rewriting the same final state) but each row removal would refuse once its row is already gone. The reviewer must rename or remove the file by hand after a --file application to keep the apply-once discipline.
Parity Relevance: Any parity implementation must reproduce this asymmetry exactly — marking in --all but not in --file — or the pending scope drifts.
Impact: src/financials/fixes.py:230-241, src/financials/fixes.py:257-267

An unconfirmable delete event still builds its mutation object (format)

Description: A failed delete (unknown id) must return "not deleted" to its caller. The function does this by constructing a complete delete event anyway — empty description, empty category, no postings, and a date of 1 January 1970 as a placeholder — and returning it alongside the False flag. Nothing is appended; the event never reaches the journal; only the caller ever sees it. It reads as odd because the natural shape is a bare False return: the zero-date artifact exists so the function can keep a uniform (event, deleted) return shape for all callers. Any future consumer that treats the returned event as meaningful without checking the flag would meet a fiction — the discipline "check the flag, not the event" lives only in the calling convention.

Parity Relevance: None
Impact: src/financials/commands.py:159-166

Confirming an unknown id falls through to the ledger before giving up (parity)

Description: The dispatcher recognizes three id shapes and hands them to their branches. Any other id — a committed-row hash id, a typo, a foreign id — lands in a final fallthrough: the ledger is loaded and the id looked up. Found: a friendly "already actual" no-op with the entry's date. Not found: the unknown-id refusal.

The quirk is the asymmetry with the typed branches: an unknown e-id gets "unknown id" at once (the register was checked), while an unknown un-typed id gets the same refusal only after a full ledger walk. Same refusal, different depth. The friendly no-op is deliberate design — re-running confirm after an interrupted attempt should reassure, not scold.

Parity Relevance: An implementation that only shape-checks ids would answer "unknown" for every committed hash id and would break the re-run reassurance path.

Impact: src/financials/confirm.py:250-261

Decline inside the occurrence editor is a cancel, not an unknown id (parity)

Description: The resolver is a chain of checks, and a failed check normally means "try the next kind, else unknown id". The occurrence editor breaks the pattern by design: because it is resolved LAST — after the ledger, the register and the groups have already declined — a decline inside it would reach the unknown-id message and tell the owner that an id they were just looking at does not exist.

The code guards this with a comment marking the resolution order as deliberate ("Resolved FIRST: a decline inside the occurrence edit is a cancel, not an unknown id" — edit.py:294-297), returning "nothing changed" for the owner's empty answer or 'q' while the menu stands. A cancel must never be misreported as a lookup failure.

Parity Relevance: None
Impact: src/financials/edit.py:294-303, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:771-772

Future routing only fires when every field is known (parity)

Description: The date test that routes entries to the expected register needs all four fields present. A one-liner like "morgen -25" (future date, amount, but no category and no description) therefore does not route forward at the shorthand stage — the capture walks the owner through the remaining questions. Only once the answers complete the entry does the branch decide: future still goes to the expected register, today or past continues to a real save.

The code comment marks this deliberately ("partial values fall through to the prompts, whose dispatch handles the future case once all fields are known"), so it is a designed two-stage dispatch rather than an accident. The oddity is only where the routing moment lives: later than a reader might expect, with the prompt itself announcing the future landing just after the date question.

Parity Relevance: A reimplementation must dispatch future entries after prompting, not before — an early router would change which register a partially stated entry lands in when the user aborts.
Impact: src/financials/entry.py:222-224, src/financials/entry.py:244-254, src/financials/entry.py:129-132

Grace lookback rescues rule instances before the ledger anchor (parity)

Description: The view's projection normally starts at the ledger's last committed entry. But a recurring instance whose day passed while the owner had not yet confirmed it would vanish behind that anchor. So the view expands rule instances starting 35 days before the anchor (or before today when nothing is committed at all). This grace is what keeps the open section complete across an out-of-order commit — a later-dated entry committed first, while an earlier instance still awaits confirmation.
Parity Relevance: None
Impact: src/financials/ledger_view.py:178-193, src/financials/recurrences.py:21-22

Group edit clears (not keeps) date, category and note on empty input (parity)

Description: The editor's rule is "Enter keeps" in general, and the label indeed keeps when left empty. But the other three fields write whatever was typed, stripped: an empty Enter clears them. So pressing Enter through the editor to "keep everything" silently wipes the group's note and unsets the rollup date and category. The owner is not warned; the update message reads the same as a real edit.
Parity Relevance: The empty-input asymmetry (label keeps, date/category/note clear) is a semantic behavior a port must decide to keep or fix explicitly, not an accident to lose.
Impact: src/financials/groups.py:364-367

Interactive annual definitions default the month to the current month (parity)

Description: Defining a yearly rule by hand, the month question is asked with the current month as its default. Enter is the accept-anywhere key throughout the definition flow, so an owner answering through it gets a commitment scheduled in the current month regardless of when the actual bill falls. The parity repair that introduced this question deliberately mirrored the day question's design — day defaults to today's day-of-month, month to today's month — so the interaction is uniform even where the silent default is thin.

The same shape holds for monthly and yearly day defaults (today's day-of-month) and for the start date (today). These defaults are ask-now-visible rather than previously-silent, so they are a design choice the parity repairs made explicit — not a leftover. Still, an owner speeding through the questions keeps a thin default: a rule that forecasts one month earlier than the bill's real month.
Parity Relevance: None
Impact: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:411-422

Owner-stated saldo silently overrides the projection (parity)

Description: During interactive capture, if the owner supplies a checking saldo value up front (or the prompt-loop confirms within tolerance), that value is not written as the row's balance — the projection recomputes it from the chain regardless. The owner-stated number only decides whether the save proceeds: match it or give the same value three times, and the ledger row's saldo is still whatever the chain produces.

The odd part cuts the other way for the flagged path — a --checking value handed in with a full set of flags is accepted and never compared against the projection at all. The owner sees the projected saldo only after saving, inside the confirmation line, not before. So the same owner-stated number is a hard gate on the interactive path and merely cosmetic on the flagged path: it neither influences the stored balance nor gets validated there. A mistyped saldo therefore sails through flagged capture and only shows up afterwards as a mismatch with the bank.

Parity Relevance: A reimplementation must decide whether --checking validates against the projection or is ignored; the code says ignored (the parameter is accepted and dropped).
Impact: src/financials/entry.py:186-187, src/financials/entry.py:261-267, src/financials/entry.py:156-177

Show exits non-zero for a group whose only members are dangling (exit-codes)

Description: The show view's exit code encodes "did anything resolve": zero when at least one member resolved into a row, non-zero when the group has members and none resolved — even though the note about dangling ids was already the owner-facing outcome. An empty group (no members ever added) also exits non-zero. The table still renders in every case, so the owner sees the picture; only the exit code differs.
Parity Relevance: A reimplementation must reproduce the exact exit-code rule (rows resolved → 0, otherwise 2, including the empty-group case) for scriptable callers to behave identically.
Impact: src/financials/groups.py:245-249

14. Sensitivity

A refused entry mid-batch wedges later apply-all runs

A corrections file with one bad entry wedges the apply-all pass: earlier entries already journaled, the file still pending, the same batch refused afresh on every later run until the file is fixed or removed by hand.

A stale register row survives a failed drop

The expected register is rewritten only after the confirm mutation has applied; if that register rewrite is interrupted, the books are correct but a stale expectation lingers — visible in the OPEN section and confirmable a second time.

Confirmation prompts are Dutch-only and vocabulary-split

The three removal paths each word confirmation differently — typ-ja free-text on two paths, a yes/no default-false question on the third — all in Dutch, so an English-speaking owner cannot reliably read what they are consenting to.

First-ledger-row dating and future-dated rows can strand the chain

An actual entry dated before the ledger's first row cannot be saved, and warning only inside the prompt is easy to miss — a future-dated confirmation rows further out the more the ledger grows.

Group membership re-pointing trusts per-member reloads

The group walk re-reads the membership file after every member commit because each commit reboots the journal; the re-pointing must find the group by id again each time or a retired member id is written back stale.

Found in: src/financials/confirm.py:159-170

Membership edits ride full-file rewrites of the group registry

Every membership change rewrites the whole group registry file, so a save racing another save — or crashing mid-write — can lose the other change; the registry has no journalling or merging.

Move-to-future is not undoable if the recreate step fails

Moving a committed entry to a future date removes it from the ledger before the new expectation exists, so a failed recreate leaves the books without the row until the owner recovers it by hand.
