    Functional Analysis

    1. Executive summary

    It is a household ledger — the archetype story calls it a double-entry-adjacent personal ledger with a forecast layer — and its whole design protects one thing: books that can prove their own history.

    1. The books replay their own history: every change joins an append-only, content-hashed journal — timestamped so even identical same-day recordings stay distinct — and every row re-verifies on read; a correction is a new recorded event, never a quiet edit.
    2. Planned money cannot corrupt recorded money: recurring rules and their forecast are an overlay never materialized into the books, and the only bridge from plan to reality is the deliberately zero-prompt confirm of a landed entry.
    3. A caution for bulk-cleanup reviews: an amount correction on a transfer row silently overwrites any custom savings side — a reviewer fixing one field should expect both sides to rewrite.
    4. Destructive actions demand a typed affirmative and offer no undo; the journal — not a restore command — is the recovery story.

    Verification status: no objection facts and no verification-phase records exist in the fact base at narration time, so these findings rest on the analyst passes' own confidence.

    Next step: read the companion product summary and archetype mapping for the capability walk and concept map, then the per-surface detail sections.

    2. Archetype mapping

    The archetype is a double-entry-adjacent personal ledger with a forecast layer: a single-household bookkeeping suite that keeps an auditable account of committed money, a parking place for planned money, and a rules engine that turns history into forecast — nothing cloud-served, no multi-user world, just one household's two accounts kept provably true. Four core concepts carry the whole shape.

    Actual versus expected is the load-bearing distinction: a movement either happened (dated today or earlier) and lives in the journaled books, or it is planned (dated ahead) and parks in a register that carries no balances. Nearly every rule and boundary in the project reads as a consequence of that one line.

    Content-hash identity is the integrity archetype: a recording's identity is a hash of its own content plus creation time, appended to a journal that can never be edited — so the books replay their own history rather than remember it.

    Running balances is the everyday payoff: every ledger row carries the balance it produced, so the household position at any date is a read, not a recalculation, and a backdated entry threads itself into the chain and pushes every later balance truthful again.

    The approved category list is budgeting policy made executable — the household's own configured vocabulary is checked fresh at every recording on both the actual and the expected side.

    Two further concepts organize the planner: a rule instance is a projected, virtual occurrence — real in the forecast, never in the books — and the confirmation backlog names what has landed but not yet been confirmed, one queue with one drain path.

    How the concepts live in the components, in the component census's own order:

    • The transaction entry interface is where actual-versus-expected is policed at the front door — it validates, routes by date, and closes the loop with the projected-balance confirmation.
    • The confirmation dispatcher is the only bridge between the two worlds: it commits known values as recorded money without a conversation.
    • The interactive entry editor moves entries across that boundary by their corrected date — future edits turn recorded rows into plans, past-dated plans book into the ledger.
    • The recurrence engine is the forecast layer proper: rule store, projection of virtual instances, and the detection that proposes rules — never writing money itself.
    • The interactive deletion dispatcher is the four-way resolver: each store owns its own removal path, and destructive steps demand a typed affirmative.
    • The journaled ledger engine realizes content-hash identity and running balances — append, replay, verify, carry balances forward.
    • The expected register store is the planned-money world: strictly future-dated, balance-free, struck the moment a plan settles.
    • The cleanup fix engine treats corrections as reviewed, offline-authored change-set files (the change-set concept), each fix guarded — a fix may state the old value the ledger must confirm, the from-guard.

    3. Product summary

    This is a household bookkeeping suite: typed commands over one household's checking and savings money, built so the books can always prove what was done to them. Everything rides one distinction — money that happened versus money that is only planned — and every capability below keeps that line sharp.

    Recording a movement is the front door. The bookkeeper gives a spend or income as a one-line quick-add or through prompts that ask for anything missing; date, description, an approved category and a signed amount are checked — the category must be on the household's approved list — and the entry routes by its date: any future-dated entry parks in the expected register instead of the actual books, while moving money between the household's own accounts touches both balances at once. On the prompted path the balance is part of the deal: the projected checking balance is confirmed before anything saves, three mismatches throw the recording away, and no hand-corrected balance can ever be stored.

    Confirming landed plans is the second door. When planned money arrives — a parked one-off entry, a due instance of a recurring rule, or a group of them — one confirm step commits the planned values with no questions asked, strikes the plan from the register, and answers an already-settled id harmlessly so nothing double-books. Adjusting values is deliberately the edit surface's job, done before confirming — never the confirm's.

    Correcting walks one pre-filled form — Enter keeps a field, re-typing changes it, and only real changes are recorded. Where the correction lands follows the new date: a recorded entry corrected to today or earlier is journaled as a full restatement so later balances stay truthful; a recorded entry pushed into the future leaves the books and becomes a plan; a plan edited into the past books itself without further questions.

    Recurring payments become standing rules. The tool scans the committed history for regularly repeating patterns, proposes them as read-only review rows — detection never creates a rule — and stores a rule only after the bookkeeper approves each detected field. An approved rule projects virtual due-dates into the forecast; it pauses, resumes, edits or retires without ever touching the books. The forecast is an overlay; only the bookkeeper's explicit confirmation of a due date creates recorded money.

    Removing an entry is deliberate and loud. A typed id resolves in exactly one store — books, register, groups or rule occurrences — the row is shown, and a typed affirmative is required; bare Enter cancels. An actual transaction's removal is itself journal-recorded with its complete prior content, so history stays provable; the design offers no undo — re-creating a wrongly removed entry means recording it anew.

    The books themselves are the spine: every business change joins an append-only journal as a mutation stamped with a creation time and a content hash, replayed into a ledger that carries each row's running balance and re-verifies every row on read. The household always sees the balance a recording produces, at any date, without recalculation.

    Bulk corrections travel as reviewed change-set files: each fix names one known row, may state the old value it expects, and lands as exactly one recorded change; pending files sweep oldest first and are renamed applied when consumed. Cleanup can correct or trim history — it can never add rows to the actual books.

    Where the product stops is design, not omission: balances are never hand-overridden at saving time, intake never asks for subcategories, confirmation never adjusts values, committed removals have no undo, and no automatic step ever writes recorded money on its own.

    6. Concepts

    Approved categories

    The household's configured set of permitted category labels — the single policy list every recording's category is checked against.

    Actual versus expected

    The household books' two-sided world — movements that happened (journaled, balance-carrying) versus movements planned (parked, balance-free).

    Running balances

    Each ledger row records the account balances that result after its postings — the household's always-current position, recomputed and carried forward from every recording.

    Content-hash identity

    A recording's provenance and integrity guarantee — what happened is identifiable by what it says, timestamped so identical sayings stay distinct.

    Category-led description

    In the quick-add line, a description written as "Kategorie: text" hands the part before the colon to the approved-category vocabulary and the rest to the description.

    Open backlog (OPEN section)

    The landed-but-unconfirmed queue — overdue plans plus due rule instances — that drain as confirmations commit them.

    Confirmation backlog

    A catch-all label for the same OPEN view, used here for the rule-instance side of the unconfirmed queue.

    Full replacement update

    The correction discipline where an update mutation replaces all four mutable fields of a recorded entry rather than recording a diff of what changed.

    Pre-image (the victim's content at removal time)

    The pre-image is the removed entry's complete content at the moment of removal, carried inside the removal record so history can rebuild and prove what was deleted.

    Rule instance (a projected occurrence of a recurring rule)

    A rule instance is a virtual row that a recurring-payment rule projects onto a future date — real in the forecast, never in the books.

    7. Business Rules

    Future-dated entries park in the expected register

    A transaction dated after today is not recorded in the actual books — it is saved as a plan (an expected entry) in a separate store with fixed status "expected" and no balance carrying, regardless of which surface the bookkeeper used. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:215-232, src/financials/entry.py:244-253, src/financials/expected.py:79-84

    New entries require an approved category

    A transaction may only be recorded under a category from the approved list; an unapproved category rejects the recording with a saving-refusal message. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:78-79, src/financials/expected.py:73-75

    An add mutation may retire an expected entry

    When the add recording names an expected-entry id to retire, the journaling command builds a confirm mutation instead of a plain add — the parked plan converts into an actual entry linked to the plan it retires. implemented in: src/financials/commands.py:108-115, src/financials/journal.py:267-284

    Transfers mirror into savings

    A movement in the transfer category posts its amount against checking and the negated amount against savings — the engine moves both balances with one recording. implemented in: src/financials/commands.py:88-95

    Prompts confirm the projected balance before saving

    On the interactive path, after typing an entry the bookkeeper is shown the projected checking balance and must confirm it (or retype it) — the projected value is derived from the ledger row the engine will chain from, and three mismatches cancel the recording. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:154-177, src/financials/entry.py:110-118

    Prompted entries chain from the date-positioned predecessor

    A new actual entry's opening balance for the projection is taken from the ledger row dated immediately before the entry's date — the same row the engine will chain from — so an inserted backdated entry re-chains balances around it. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:154-159, src/financials/entry.py:87-90, src/financials/journal.py:220-226

    Every mutation is content-hash identified and journal-verified

    Each business change is recorded as a mutation whose identity is a content hash of its canonical form plus a creation timestamp; it is appended to an append-only journal, and the ledger rebuilt from it verifies hash and timestamps on read. implemented in: src/financials/journal.py:9-24, src/financials/commands.py:72-85, src/financials/journal.py:124-133

    Register entries require a strictly future date

    The expected register accepts only entries dated strictly after today — its own direct surface refuses any recording dated today or earlier and points the bookkeeper back to the add surface. implemented in: src/financials/expected.py:79-84

    Abort leaves nothing stored

    Every cancellation point in the intake — a refusal at validation, an aborted prompt ('q'), a Ctrl-C interrupt, a failed shorthand parse, or three failed balance confirmations — ends the session with nothing written to any store. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:240-242, src/financials/entry.py:256-259, src/financials/entry.py:176-177, src/financials/cli.py:288-290

    A zero amount never saves on the actual-ledger intake

    A transaction routed to the actual books with amount 0 is rejected on every actual-side path — flagged, prompted and shorthand alike — with an explicit refusal message. implemented in: src/financials/entry.py:80-81, src/financials/entry.py:148-151, src/financials/shorthand.py:122-125

    8. Process Flows

    Transaction intake

    Trigger: A spend or income happens and the household bookkeeper must record it (surface: the financials add command)

    Open-entry confirmation

    Trigger: A planned payment (expected entry or recurring-rule instance) has actually landed and must be committed as paid (surface: the financials confirm command)

    Entry revision

    Trigger: A recorded or planned entry contains wrong data and must be corrected (surface: the financials edit command)

    Entry removal

    Trigger: Surface: the financials delete command — a recorded transaction or planned entry must be removed from the books.

    Recurring payment provisioning

    Trigger: The bookkeeper turns a repeating payment pattern in the history into a forecastable recurring rule (surface: the financials recurrence detect | add commands)

    Ledger cleanup-fx application

    Trigger: Reviewed correction change-sets for historical data are pending and must reach the ledger (surface: the financials fix command)

    12. Non-Goals

    No balance override at intake

    Statement: The intake deliberately offers no path to save a recording whose balance deviates from the projected books balance — the figure is confirmed or the recording is cancelled.
    Rationale: The confirmation gate's design: derived balances only. Whatever the bookkeeper types in the New checking-balance prompt, accepting is only possible within half a cent of the projection; deviating values are treated as failed confirmations, and three failures cancel. There is no "save anyway with my figure" branch, and no flag to bypass the gate. Whether an override door is wanted at all is a hotspot's question — but as built, the intake's answer is no.

    No subcategory prompt at intake

    Statement: The intake never asks for or stores a subcategory — new recordings carry empty subcategory fields even though the row shape and shorthand 'Category: description' form could host one.
    Rationale: The row shape carries a split-category (category plus optional subcategory kept from the retired importer), and the shorthand grammar's 'Head: tail' form could carry a subcategory — but intake assigns none. The prompt walks date, description, category, amount (and the balance confirmation) — no subcategory step exists, and the stored new entry's subcategory fields stay empty. Any subcategory discipline is a later stage's concern, not intake's.

    Confirmation never asks to adjust values

    Statement: The one-step confirm never prompts for date, description, category or amount of a landed plan; the interactive adjustments only exist on the separate future-instance path, not as an option of the landed confirm.
    Rationale: The confirm surface's own help text draws the boundary: confirm as-is, zero prompts, and "Adjust first: 'financials edit'". Even a landed plan's values arrive at the commit untouched — if the plan was wrong, the household workflow is to edit the plan, then confirm it. The only interactive confirm is the future-instance path (confirming an early-landed instance whose rule values need a real date/amount), which is a different business case, not a bypass of this boundary. The design keeps the fast path fast and puts all value corrections behind the edit door.

    Confirming writes no per-instance store

    Statement: Confirming a rule instance does not materialize or store the instance anywhere — only the ordinary booking exists after the commit, plus possibly an exception date on the rule.
    Rationale: The recurrence design keeps instances virtual: they are projected from rules for display, never stored as rows. Confirmation respects that boundary — the confirm books real money and never records "this instance was confirmed" except through the booking itself (and, when the booking drifted, an exception date on the rule so the stale projection leaves). There is deliberately no instance table, no confirmation log per instance, and no per-instance flag; the books' covering booking IS the confirmation record.

    The edit flow never edits rule instances or groups in place

    Statement: The edit resolver hands group ids and recurrence-instance ids to their own dedicated editors rather than correcting them through the transaction edit form.
    Rationale: When the id handed to edit resolves to a group or a rule instance, the resolver hands off — the group editor handles label, rollup date, category and note, and the occurrence editor handles the two-option rule-versus-exception choice. The transaction edit form (the four-field prompted pass) never edits these shapes in place. Deliberate boundary, not an omission: rule instances carry their own drift and exception rules, groups carry membership — both need different questions than the transaction form asks. The hand-off happens before the unknown-id refusal, so neither shape errors out of the edit surface.

    No undo for a committed removal

    Statement: The books deliberately offer no undo of a committed removal — the journal's audit trail is the only record of what was removed, and no command reverses it.
    Rationale: The delete module's docstring states the boundary plainly — there is no undo; the journal is the audit trail of what was removed. Re-creating a wrongly removed transaction means adding it again as a new entry, not restoring the old one. This is a deliberate design choice, not an oversight — the removal record with its full pre-image is the recoverability story. The household that wants an entry back re-enters it; the journal stays append-only in both directions.

    Recurring rules are never materialized into the transaction store

    Statement: The system deliberately never writes the recurring rules' projected instances into the actual books or any persistent instance store — instances exist only as the view-time projection, until a real booking supersedes or an explicit confirmation books one.
    Rationale: Two boundaries define the rule engine's honesty, both stated as owner decisions in the code's own words. The rule engine's module header: rules are never materialized into the transaction store — instance rows are computed fresh inside each view window and vanish with it. The command surface's header: the rule book is the recurring analogue of the expected register — overlay configuration, not journaled, because the journal is the story of committed money only. Together this means approving a rule, pausing it, or even the forecast showing a due bill never touches the books; only the bookkeeper's explicit confirmation of an instance creates journal entries, through the same doors as any booking. The forecast layer cannot corrupt the ledger by existing.

    A cleanup change-set never adds rows to the actual books

    Statement: The whole-row action set a cleanup file may use contains only removal of recorded rows, clearing of parked plans and parking of new planned entries — no action introduces a new row into the actual books.
    Rationale: A fix file can correct a row's fields or strike a row, and it can park a planned entry into the expected register (also a route for the interactive flows' internal ops list). It cannot create a new recorded transaction: no row-adding action exists in the change-set vocabulary, and the row engine refuses any action name outside its set. The boundary keeps cleanup files from becoming a second entry path — recorded history grows only at the entry interface, while cleanup only fixes or trims it.

    13. Quirks

    Balance mismatch retries then cancels without storing a correction (parity)

    Description: When the interactive session shows the projected checking balance and the bookkeeper disagrees with it (they know the real position), they can retype the correct balance — but the code only accepts the confirmation when the typed figure matches the projection within half a cent. A deliberate correction — "I know the balance is really X" — is treated as a failed attempt: warned "does not match", and after three attempts the entire recording is thrown away. There is no path to save an entry with a balance override; the only surviving choice is to accept the projection or to record nothing.
    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/entry.py:160-177

    First-entry date strictly after the last row is silently required (parity)

    Description: A recording whose date precedes every existing row cannot compute a balance base, and the code refuses it with a generic "no preceding row" message. On the interactive path the session shows the last row's date and a warning when that row is in the future (advising a later date) — but nothing guides the very first entry into an empty ledger: there, the refusal is bare, with no hint that "opening" rows are the intended fix. A bookkeeper landing here gets no suggestion that the opening position should be recorded first.
    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/entry.py:87-90, src/financials/entry.py:114-118

    The re-pointed member id wins over the original even on an unapplied commit (parity)

    Description: Inside the group confirm loop, the membership update for a confirmed plan member reassigns through a chained conditional built like "the new committed identifier, or the original — with the original spelled out on both halves of the branch". As written, the fallback that should keep the old id actually produces an empty string whenever the new id is absent, because the branch structure evaluates to the not-the-original arm regardless. Today the loop only re-points after _commit_expected returned a committed entry with an id, so the blank arm is unreachable in practice — but the expression will silently blank a member out of the group if that guard ever loosens. The safe form — new id when present, otherwise the original — is one nesting level off from what sits there.

    Parity Relevance: Any re-implementation of the group-confirm member re-point keeps the plain "new id if present, else old id" intent, not the odd branch spelling.
    Impact: src/financials/confirm.py:169

    A covered-instance skip inside group confirm checks only the checking posting (parity)

    Description: Before committing a rule-instance member of a group, the code checks whether the books already hold a booking that covers the instance (same category, amount within a cent, date inside the window). That check, as spelled there, insists the covering booking carries a checking posting — and only the checking posting's amount is compared to the rule amount. The standalone view's own covering test compares the row's amount without that posting requirement. A rule projected on savings-side movements could therefore be re-committed by a group confirm even though a matching booking already exists on the savings side. In household practice every commit posts to checking, so the difference is latent, but the two covering tests are not the same rule as written.

    Parity Relevance: Parity work must decide which covering test is canonical — the shared window/tolerance match, or the checking-posting-bound variant the group path spells — before copying either.
    Impact: src/financials/confirm.py:183-189, src/financials/recurrences.py:110-125

    The instance-confirm id window hides landed instances older than the lookback (exit-codes)

    Description: Confirming by instance id searches the active rules' projected instances inside a bounded window: from the ledger's anchor date minus a grace lookback, to today plus about thirteen months. An instance whose due date fell further in the past than that window — a bill nobody confirmed for weeks — is simply not found: the confirm call behaves as if the id were unknown. The same bounded reach applies to group members resolved through the instance path. The books themselves are the fallback (their covering superseding), but the direct one-step confirm of a long-ignored instance is not possible; the bookkeeper would book the payment the ordinary way instead.

    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:569-593, src/financials/recurrences.py:20-22

    Empty edit of an unlanded plan reports nothing changed with no follow-up (parity)

    Description: Two nearly identical sessions diverge by one date comparison. On a landed plan the untouched form books the plan (confirm intent); on a today-or-earlier check at edit.py:147 the branch is date-gated, and a future plan reaches the separate nothing-changed branch at edit.py:150-152, whose only output is '[dim]Niets gewijzigd.[/dim]' and a 0 exit. The bookkeeper gets no suggestion the landed case would have confirmed — the behavior gap is invisible until they try the same thing tomorrow. The code is deciding this consistently, so it is an oddity rather than an open question — but the asymmetry reads as unintended; the all-Enter confirm exists only where the date happens to have landed.

    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/edit.py:147-152

    Removing a transfer row warns about the savings chain (integrity)

    Description: A transfer row moves money between the checking and savings accounts — its removal therefore touches both account chains, not just checking. The command detects the transfer's category after the deletion succeeds and prints a yellow note that removing a transfer also affects the savings chain, advising the bookkeeper to review the ledger listing afterwards. This is a heads-up, not a gate — the deletion has already been applied when the warning is shown, and no follow-up action is forced.
    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/delete.py:69-73

    The bare delete picker shows only past rows (parity)

    Description: The bare delete command shows the committed view's last ten rows as a menu and asks which to delete. But the picker filters on date, not on kind — it takes rows whose date is not in the future. Committed entries are dated in the past or today, so the filter's real effect lands on anything unusual rather than on future-dated plan rows; plan rows carry plan-numbered ids the resolver would route to the expected register anyway. The oddity is the filter's bluntness — a date window standing where a kind check would read more naturally. The bookkeeper-visible effect is small — plan and rule rows are simply absent from the menu they pick from.
    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/delete.py:77-92

    A rule that already exists is appended again on every approval (parity)

    Description: The detector's dedup rule (never re-propose a covered pattern) is the only dedup the flow has. The hand-add path, proposal adoption and even the edit path simply append the (possibly edited) rule object to the loaded list and save — no uniqueness check on description plus category anywhere on the write side. The bookkeeper can therefore store two rules with identical pattern fields by adding twice or by editing a rule's description into another rule's description; both will project their instances into every future period, doubling the forecast. The superseding matcher softens the visual symptom slightly (a covering booking retires one instance of each), but two identical rules stay on the books as two. The collision refusal lives only in the vault, so to speak — not in the store.
    Parity Relevance: A parity port must decide whether uniqueness is checked at write time or trusted to the detector's read-time exclusion; the current code trusts it.
    Impact: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:464-466, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:946-948, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:561-562

    The yearly proposal day rides the last occurrence of the pattern (parity)

    Description: When the detector composes a proposal, the start field is the group's first occurrence (the phase anchor), but the day and month schedule fields come from the LAST occurrence in the sorted list. For a monthly pattern that drifted across month boundaries (e.g. paid the 31st, then the 30th, then the 1st), the proposed day is the most recent month's placement, and for yearly patterns likewise the latest month/day pair. The bookkeeper reviewing the table sees "Eerst" (first occurrence) next to a day-of-month derived from the end of the series — the proposal is internally consistent in cadence, but its day may not match its own first occurrence. Nothing wrong is stored (the approval loop shows the day as an editable default), but the displayed pair can puzzle at a glance.
    Parity Relevance: None
    Impact: src/financials/recurrences.py:293-300

    15. Sensitivity

    Date comparison as ISO strings

    Every future/past dispatch decision in the intake compares ISO date strings lexically, not calendar dates — any non-padded or malformed date string can flip the routing decision, and only the later date-format validation catches the malformed cases.
    Found in: src/financials/entry.py:220, src/financials/entry.py:126-128, src/financials/entry.py:73-75, src/financials/expected.py:66-69

    Balance projection must agree with the engine's chaining

    The interactive confirmation shows a projected checking balance derived from the ledger's date-positioned predecessor, and it must be computed with the same logic the engine will apply on saving — any divergence shows the bookkeeper a figure the engine will not produce.
    Found in: src/financials/entry.py:154-158, src/financials/commands.py:179-192

    The register strike must never precede a successful commit

    The delete of the plan row happens only after the confirming booking has applied and returned a committed row; striking first would destroy a plan whose payment never got recorded.
    Found in: src/financials/confirm.py:39-43, src/financials/confirm.py:46-47, src/financials/confirm.py:57-61

    Instance resolution depends on the ledger's anchor row

    Which rule instances confirm can see depends on where the ledger's anchor date currently sits — a long-unconfirmed ledger shifts the reachable window, and the confirm paths inherit that sensitivity.
    Found in: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:569-593, src/financials/recurrences.py:20-22

    The move-to-expected hole can leave an entry in neither store

    The move path deletes the committed entry before creating the planned replacement, so a rejection at the second step destroys the entry with no automatic undo — only a manual rebuild via the expect command.
    Found in: src/financials/edit.py:213-229

    Transfer edits and moves must keep the savings mirror truthful

    Any edit or move of a transfer (Overdracht) touches two account chains — the checking posting and the mirrored savings posting — and the planned register holds only the checking shape, so moves need an explicit savings-side check.
    Found in: src/financials/commands.py:88-95, src/financials/edit.py:231-235, src/financials/edit.py:266-270

    Rule suppression matches by description and category

    The suppression writes the exception onto every rule whose description and category both match the scanned instance — a same-worded pair of rules would take the exception together.
    Found in: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:839-843

    Stale-pre-image refusal halts the entire boot

    A mismatching removal record does not only block its own deletion — the value error rises through the replay and the books refuse to open at all.
    Found in: src/financials/journal.py:323-346, src/financials/journal.py:526-532, src/financials/journal.py:516-520

    Proposal numbering resolves only against a fresh detector run

    Adopting a proposal by number recomputes the proposal table at adoption time — the number is only meaningful when history and existing rules have not changed since the table was shown.
    Found in: src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:914-927, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:860-862

    Whole-file rule overwrite keeps every writer honest about reload-then-save

    Every rule mutation reloads the store from disk immediately before rewriting it — a writer that edited an in-memory list from an earlier read would silently erase changes another command made in between.
    Found in: src/financials/recurrences.py:49-54, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:464-466, src/financials/recurrence_cli.py:528
